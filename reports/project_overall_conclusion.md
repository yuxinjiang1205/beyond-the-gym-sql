After completing this project, I was quite surprised by some of the results. More importantly, this project helped me understand how my lifestyle and daily activity patterns changed across different stages of my life.

Looking back at my original project proposal, I used to feel extremely guilty whenever I skipped the gym, regardless of the reason. At the time, I struggled to find a healthy balance between exercise and other aspects of my life. I often assumed that missing a gym session meant having a less active day. However, the results challenged this assumption. Although Gym Days generally recorded higher daily active calorie expenditure, 60 out of 280 Non-Gym Days exceeded the Gym Day median. In addition, Non-Gym Days during the selected extended travel periods recorded higher average daily active calories than ordinary Non-Gym Days.

The differences in average daily active calorie expenditure across the three life stages were broadly consistent with my expectations. Canada recorded the highest average, Taiwan the lowest, and Scotland fell between the two. My daily routines and responsibilities also changed considerably across these periods, as documented in `RQ3_background.md`.

During my time in Canada, I rarely used public transport for everyday activities such as commuting to work, grocery shopping, and going to the gym. Canada also recorded the longest average strength-training duration among the three life stages.

Although I knew that my daily routine in Canada involved more walking and longer gym sessions, I initially assumed that my gym attendance in Taiwan was relatively similar. Therefore, I expected my daily active calorie expenditure to be lower in Taiwan, but not by such a large margin. Surprisingly, Taiwan recorded a much higher strength-training frequency (84.14%) than Canada (57.46%), yet its average daily active calorie expenditure was considerably lower. As an additional check, excluding the two extended travel periods from the Canada stage increased its recorded strength-training frequency to approximately 65.66%, which was still considerably lower than Taiwan's 84.14%.

Living with my parents in Taiwan also meant that I rarely needed to run errands as frequently as I did in Canada. These findings made me realise that I might have underestimated the contribution of everyday activities outside the gym to my overall daily active calorie expenditure. However, the available data cannot determine how much these activities contributed to the differences between the two life stages.

A similar pattern emerged in RQ4. Before conducting the analysis, I was already aware that I had travelled to Stirling city centre less frequently during the Intensive Dissertation period. However, I also assumed that my gym attendance had decreased considerably. Surprisingly, the results showed relatively modest differences in recorded strength-training frequency and duration between the Early and Intensive Dissertation periods. In contrast, mean and median daily active calorie expenditure decreased by 9.46% and 12.73%, respectively. Furthermore, the supplementary analysis showed that mean daily active calorie expenditure was lower during the Intensive Dissertation period on both days with and without recorded strength training.

There are several limitations to this project that need to be addressed. First, this project was based on my personal Apple Health data, so the findings may not be representative of individuals with different lifestyles.

Second, active calorie expenditure is an estimated value, while recorded workout duration does not necessarily reflect actual training intensity. The available data do not capture factors such as the weights lifted or the number of repetitions performed.

Finally, although I recalled detailed life experiences across different stages, the analysis did not directly measure how individual lifestyle factors contributed to changes in activity levels. Therefore, the findings cannot establish causal relationships between these factors and the observed differences in daily active calorie expenditure.

Together, these findings suggest that gym attendance is a useful indicator of structured exercise, but it does not fully represent overall daily physical activity. Although recorded strength-training days were associated with higher daily active calorie expenditure, activities outside the gym also need to be considered when interpreting daily activity patterns. My results also suggest that differences in daily routines and living environments may help explain variations in physical activity across different life stages. Overall, this project has encouraged me to look beyond gym attendance when evaluating my daily activity levels.
