## 1. Research Question and Purpose

**How did physical activity patterns differ across periods of living in Canada, Taiwan, and Scotland?**

RQ3 examines daily active calorie expenditure, recorded strength-training frequency, and changes in activity patterns across three periods of my life. These periods differed in employment, academic commitments, transportation, access to fitness facilities, and everyday routines. Documenting those circumstances before analysing the data provides a basis for interpreting the eventual findings without assuming in advance which period was the most active.

This document draws primarily on my recollections of daily life, alongside dates I have confirmed. It is intended as contextual background for the RQ3 analysis, not as a record of quantitative results. The relevant patterns and comparisons will be established through SQL using Apple Health data.

## 2. Definition of the Life Stages

The periods were defined around major personal transitions rather than strict geographical presence on every recorded day.

| Life stage | Start date | End date | Main circumstances |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canada | 2024-09-06 | 2025-04-22 | Full-time retail work, IELTS preparation, and travel before returning to Taiwan |
| Taiwan | 2025-04-23 | 2025-09-14 | Part-time English teaching and preparation for postgraduate study |
| Scotland | 2025-09-15 | 2026-10-27 | MSc study, dissertation work, and relocation from Stirling to Glasgow |

These boundaries were agreed before examining the RQ3 results. Travel within a life stage will remain part of that stage because the research concerns broader periods of life, not only days physically spent within a particular country. Accordingly, the Italy trip from 6 to 22 April 2025 is assigned to the Canada stage as the final part of the transition before my return to Taiwan on 23 April. The day I departed Taiwan for Scotland, 14 September 2025, is assigned to the Taiwan stage; Scotland begins on my arrival on 15 September. These are analytical classifications, not claims about my physical location on each day.

## 3. Contextual Background

### 3.1 Canada: Retail Employment and IELTS Preparation

I lived in Banff, Alberta, where I worked full-time as a sales associate in a gift shop in Banff National Park. The role involved much more than operating a till: I regularly assisted customers, restocked and organised merchandise, and moved around the shop throughout my shifts. I generally worked four or five days per week, for approximately seven to eight hours per shift. Around January 2025, I arranged to work about four days per week so that I could devote more time to IELTS preparation, although I occasionally still worked five days. My last working day was 2 April 2025.

Walking was central to everyday life in Banff. I rarely used public transport and generally walked to work, the library, shops, and the gym. My regular gym, Sally Borden Fitness and Recreation at the Banff Centre, was uphill from town, so the journey involved climbing a slope rather than walking on level ground. During a temporary closure of that facility, I used the Fenlands Banff Recreation Centre and purchased a ten-entry pass; I have not confirmed how many entries I actually used. These journeys, together with movement at work, meant that my routine included physical activity outside formal exercise sessions.

Strength training was also a major part of my routine. I recall attending the gym on most days, including after work, and often training for more than an hour, sometimes around 90 minutes. I particularly remember focusing on lower-body exercises such as barbell back squats. I started using an Apple Watch on 6 September 2024, but did not join the gym until approximately 28 September. During those earlier weeks, I exercised at home with dumbbells. This distinction will matter when interpreting recorded strength-training days: an exercise record does not necessarily demonstrate attendance at a gym, and the absence of a gym membership does not imply inactivity.

Following a trip to France and London in December 2024, I combined retail work and regular exercise with intensive IELTS study. I spent most days off studying at the Banff Public Library and sat the IELTS examination in Canada on **21 March 2025**. The shift towards more study time, alongside the reduction in working days, represented an important change in my daily routine. After leaving my job, I departed Calgary for Italy on 6 April 2025 and travelled there until 22 April. For RQ3, this trip is retained within the Canada life stage, although it took place outside Canada.

### 3.2 Taiwan: Part-Time Teaching and Postgraduate Preparation

I returned to Taiwan on 23 April 2025 and quickly began preparing university applications while looking for part-time work. I received an offer from the University of Stirling on 1 May and started working as an English teacher at a cram school around 5 May 2025. My usual schedule was approximately four days per week, from 16:00 to 20:00, equivalent to about 16 working hours per week. This was a substantial change from the longer, more physically mobile retail shifts in Banff. My final teaching day was 15 August 2025.

My commute involved several modes of transport. I cycled to an MRT station, travelled two or three stops by train, and then walked for roughly ten to fifteen minutes to the school. My father usually collected me by motorcycle after work, so the return journey involved considerably less active travel. The gym was about a ten-minute walk from home along relatively flat streets, unlike the uphill approach to my gym in Banff.

After a short break of two or three days following my return to Taiwan, I resumed regular gym visits. I remember training on most days for much of the period. However, during approximately the final two weeks before leaving for Scotland, I reduced my training because I felt unusually fatigued. On some occasions I still visited the gym but undertook little or no substantial strength training. This is a recollection of my training behaviour and perceived fatigue, not a confirmed diagnosis of overreaching or a verified count of recorded workouts.

From around 18–19 June 2025, I also spent time at home preparing for postgraduate study before my afternoon shifts. I taught myself Excel to build confidence with numerical and analytical concepts and practised English academic writing with feedback from ChatGPT. These activities involved computer-based study and changed how I spent my time outside work and training. After finishing my teaching job in mid-August, I mainly prepared for the move, met friends, and continued exercising at a reduced intensity towards the end of the period. I departed Taiwan on 14 September 2025.

### 3.3 Scotland: Postgraduate Study and Changing Routines

I arrived in Scotland on 15 September 2025 to begin an MSc in Artificial Intelligence at the University of Stirling. I lived in Beech Court, on the university campus, until 29 August 2026 and did not have paid employment during the Scotland study period. My daily commitments centred on coursework, independent study, and later my dissertation. The university Sports Centre was a short walk from my accommodation, making the gym relatively easy to access. I initially maintained a regular training routine during September to November 2025.

Over time, my exercise habits became less consistent. I recall several periods of illness and fatigue between late 2025 and early 2026 and began to question whether my training load and academic pressure were affecting recovery. I subsequently reduced both workout frequency and intensity. These events provide context for interpreting possible changes in the records, but the data cannot establish that training caused the illnesses. My weekends also changed: from approximately December 2025, I travelled to Glasgow by train to see my partner almost every week, and I recall rarely attending the gym on Saturdays or Sundays.

Daily activity in Stirling was not limited to the gym. I walked around campus but generally took a bus into the city centre for larger grocery shops, particularly at Waitrose or Marks & Spencer. These trips usually involved additional walking and browsing rather than shopping and returning immediately. I used the campus shop for occasional smaller purchases. This combination of campus walking, bus travel, shopping, and regular journeys to Glasgow shaped my routine while I was studying.

I began working on my dissertation around May 2026, with the most demanding period occurring from late June through August. During those months, I remember prioritising academic work and attending the gym less often, although the extent of any change remains to be tested against the workout records. The dissertation and subsequent relocation will be examined in greater detail in RQ4; here, they form part of the wider Scotland life stage.

On 29 August 2026, I moved out of Beech Court and began living mainly in Glasgow. I returned to Stirling about three times for dental appointments, but my everyday life was based in Glasgow. I did not attend a gym during the initial moving period and recall registering at a new facility around 8 September. Meanwhile, moving belongings, walking between my accommodation and my partner’s home, and visiting the city centre involved considerable time on foot. I moved again around 18 September. From 25 September to 8 October, I travelled with my family in London and other parts of the UK; those dates remain within the Scotland stage.

My first gym in Glasgow was approximately a 30-minute walk from home. On 23 October 2026, I cancelled that membership and switched to a gym about ten minutes away on foot. Because this happened only four days before the study period ended, it is best retained as background rather than treated as a separate before-and-after comparison. Across the Scotland stage, both gym accessibility and everyday travel patterns changed substantially, from living on campus in Stirling to walking around Glasgow as part of independent city life.

## 4. Implications for the RQ3 Analysis

The three stages differed not only in formal exercise opportunities but also in the activity associated with work, travel, study, and daily errands. In Canada, physically mobile retail work and walking-based transport coincided with frequent strength training. In Taiwan, shorter teaching shifts and home-based study were combined with regular gym attendance and a mixed-mode commute. In Scotland, academic study replaced paid employment, while training habits, weekend travel, and accommodation changed over time. These contrasts offer possible lines of interpretation once the descriptive results are available; they do not establish the causes of any observed differences.

The analysis will compare daily active calorie expenditure, the frequency or proportion of days with recorded Traditional Strength Training, and activity patterns over time. Because the stages vary in length, raw counts of training days should be interpreted alongside an appropriately defined frequency measure. As in RQ1 and RQ2, a recorded `HKWorkoutActivityTypeTraditionalStrengthTraining` session is an operational indicator of strength training, **not a verified gym entry**. Recollections such as “almost daily” attendance and estimated workout durations will be checked against recorded data where possible rather than presented as established statistical findings.

The earlier Data Quality Assessment identified a missing daily activity record on 14 April 2025, during the Italy trip. That date will not be assigned an invented activity value. Existing recorded observations, including zero or unusual measurements, will be treated consistently with prior data-quality decisions. Any differences across stages will be discussed as descriptive associations within my personal data, without attributing them solely to a particular country, occupation, commute, or life event.

This background was documented before the RQ3 analysis to make the classification decisions explicit and to preserve contextual information that may help explain, challenge, or qualify the eventual findings.
